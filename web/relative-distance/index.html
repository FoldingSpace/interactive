Vancouver, measured in minutes

Press Metres, then Minutes, and watch the streets move: the map stops measuring the ground and starts measuring the trip. Change who is travelling, or where they start, and you get a different city.

Measured in

In metres this is an ordinary map. In minutes every street slides along the line running out from the start, to a distance set by how long the trip takes. The direction a place lies in never changes. Only how far away it is.

There are two maps here and no map in between, so the change is shown as a movement rather than as a dial. Half way between metres and minutes would be half of one measurement added to half of another, which is not a way of measuring anything.

The map in minutes is true along the lines that run out from the start, and only along those. The gap you see between two places that are not the start is not their travel time, and nothing on this map can show it. That is the trade a map projection makes too: to get one thing right, it gives up the rest.

For more, see: David O’Sullivan, Computing Geographically (Guilford, 2024), Chapter 2 on relative space and Figure 6.14, which is a map of this kind for Santa Barbara. Alain L’Hostis & Farouk Abdou, “What is the shape of geographical time-space?”, ISPRS International Journal of Geo-Information 10(5), 340 (2021).

Who is travelling

Nobody’s trip is this fast. There are no traffic lights here, no queues, no waiting to cross, and no time spent parking. Every traveller on this page moves as if the way were clear, because there is no open record of Vancouver traffic that could say otherwise. What the map shows is how the ground and the network shape a trip, not how long the trip takes.

Walking speed comes from Waldo Tobler’s hiking function, which is fastest on ground falling gently away and slower both uphill and steeply down. It gives 5 km/h on the level. Tobler fitted it for walking on paths in hilly country rather than on city pavement, so what it is really lending this map is the shape of the curve.

Cycling speed is a rider putting 150 watts through the pedals against gravity, rolling resistance and the air, with 90 kg of rider and bike, and never going over 22 km/h, which is the rider’s own choice rather than physics. Driving speed is the posted limit. A traveller on steps moves at 1.5 km/h.

For more, see: Waldo Tobler, “Three presentations on geographical analysis and modeling”, NCGIA Technical Report 93-1 (1993), where the hiking function is W = 6 exp(−3.5 |S + 0.05|) km/h, with S the rise over the run. David Gordon Wilson & Theodor Schmidt, with contributions by Jim Papadopoulos, Bicycling Science (4th edn, MIT Press, 2020), chapter 4, for the power equation this simplifies. On the slope limit: 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design §403.3, and CSA B651, both of which set 1:20; a ramp may go to 1:12, and needs handrails once it rises past 150 mm.

Starting from

Or tap the map anywhere. Drag a pin to move it.

Swapping the start with the blue pin is the test worth running. If getting there and getting back took the same time, the two maps would be the same map. They are not. One-way streets and hills both run one way, so the city is a different shape depending on which end of the trip you are standing at.

For more, see: David O’Sullivan, Computing Geographically (Guilford, 2024), Chapter 2: “It might take longer to travel from A to B than from B to A.”

Ground

The photograph is stretched by the same rule as the streets, so you can watch real ground pull apart. It is only stretched where the streets can say how long it takes to get there. Water and mountainside have no answer, so they stay where they are and fade out as the map turns to minutes.

That fading is the honest part. Interpolating a travel time across the inlet would make the water stretch like everything else, which would say the sea is merely slow. It is not slow. It is not there.

Turn it off if the streets are hard to read. On a projector, which crushes dark tones, plain often wins.
