A map is never a view from nowhere

Someone chose the areas. The areas decide what can be seen.

Here the police records stay fixed and everything else moves. Cut Vancouver into 996 pieces, or into 118. Shift the boundaries without changing how many there are. Change what counts as a neighbour. Each one changes the answer, and none of them is in the data. Geographers call this the modifiable areal unit problem.

The records are a standpoint too. They mark where officers were, what someone reported, and how it was written down. A crime map describes a city and also shapes what can be said about it.

Two questions, then. What does the model say? And what had to happen for these numbers to exist?

How much of the pattern the model explains —

This is R2, written here as a percentage. It is the share of the differences between areas that the two predictors account for. At 0 per cent the model tells you nothing. At 100 per cent it would predict every area exactly.

A higher R2 does not mean a better description of the world. Grouping areas together usually raises it. Fotheringham and Wong grouped 871 Buffalo block groups into fewer and fewer zones: fit averaged about 0.4 at 800 zones and about 0.85 at 100. Their conclusion was that you can reach any level of accuracy you like by aggregating enough.

Where the lines go matters as much as how many there are. Openshaw took the 99 counties of Iowa and searched for the 6-region grouping that would give him the answer he wanted. He could drive the correlation anywhere from −0.99 to +0.99. He called it applied gerrymandering. Even without searching, ordinary six-region groupings of the same data ranged from 0.26 to 0.86.

For more, see: Openshaw, S. (1984) The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem. Concepts and Techniques in Modern Geography 38. Norwich: Geo Books. · Fotheringham, A. S. and Wong, D. W. S. Reading the row

One equation for the whole city. It predicts how many incidents of the chosen kind were reported in an area, from two things: how many private households the area holds, and the median household income there, in thousands of dollars.

Each number in front of a predictor says how much the prediction changes when that predictor goes up by one, holding the other still. A term is crossed out when it could easily have come out that size by chance (p is 0.05 or more), which is the usual reason to leave it out of the equation you write down.

The equation describes areas, not people. Nobody in this data is a person. Reading a coefficient as a fact about residents is the ecological fallacy, and it is one of the things this page is built to make visible.

The income term is where that goes wrong most easily. A negative coefficient does not say that people with lower incomes steal or damage more. It says areas whose residents report lower median incomes hold more records. Those areas also differ in what the land is used for, how many people pass through in a day, how much of the space is public, and how closely it is policed.

The count is of who lives here. Most of these incidents happen where people are, not where they sleep. Work on Vancouver found that using the population actually present rather than the resident population changed which places looked like hot spots, and for several kinds of incident they nearly disappeared.

For more, see: Andresen, M. A. and Brantingham, P. J. (2007) Hot Spots of Crime in Vancouver and their Relationship with Population Characteristics. Ottawa: Department of Justice Canada.

Are the errors clustered in space? (Moran's I)
—

The model's errors should be scattered. If areas where it guesses too low sit next to other areas where it guesses too low, something with a geography to it is missing from the model.

Moran's I measures that clustering: near 0 means scattered, positive means neighbours resemble each other. Neighbours here are areas that touch, each counted equally.

The usual reading of clustered errors is that a variable with a geography is missing from the model. Recording and policing are two such variables: where officers are sent, how readily an incident is reported, and how it is written up all vary across a city. Part of what clusters here is the recording rather than the thing recorded, and no amount of care with the statistics separates them.

The p-value comes from shuffling the errors between areas 999 times and asking how often chance alone beats what we see. Treat it as a rough guide: the errors are left over from a model already fitted to this data, so the shuffling is not quite testing what it appears to.

For more, see: Cliff, A. D. and Ord, J. K. (1972) Testing for spatial autocorrelation among regression residuals. Geographical Analysis 4(3), 267–284. · Cliff, A. D. and Ord, J. K. (1973) Spatial Autocorrelation. London: Pion.

Dissemination areas and census tracts are real units published by Statistics Canada. A tract is built from whole dissemination areas, so the two are the same city counted at two grains.

The random areas are grown from random starting points until each holds about the same number of households, which is roughly how the real units are built. They are drawn only to answer one question: if the number of areas stays the same and only the boundaries move, does the answer move too?

It does. That is the modifiable areal unit problem, and it has two halves. Going from 996 areas to 118 is the scale problem. Pressing redraw, with the number of areas held still, is what they called the aggregation problem, now often called the zoning effect.

For more, see: Openshaw, S. and Taylor, P. J. (1979) A million or so correlation coefficients: three experiments on the modifiable areal unit problem. In N. Wrigley (ed.) Statistical Applications in the Spatial Sciences. London: Pion, 127–144.

Reports of

These count incidents reported to police and recorded under each heading in 2015 and 2016. A record exists only if someone reported it, an officer wrote it down, and it was filed under this heading. None of those three happens at the same rate everywhere, or for every kind of incident.

The headings themselves were written by an institution for its own purposes. Change one and the city changes shape. Some of what moves is how each kind of incident comes to be written down at all: a stolen bicycle often goes unreported, a break-in at a business is reported because a claim needs a file number. Read the difference between the categories as evidence about the recording as much as about the city.

Mischief in Canadian law covers damage to property, graffiti included. Whether an act is written up under that heading depends on who complains, whose property was involved, and how an officer files it.

Residential break and enter is missing on purpose. It is the category the GEOG 370 lab asks you to model, and this page is meant to sharpen what you make of your own results rather than hand them to you.

The model

Each area on its own fits one equation from an area's own households and income. Its errors cluster in space, which says something with a geography is missing from it.

What is left out has a shape takes that seriously. It assumes the missing things are themselves smooth over space, estimates how smooth, and refits the equation with that structure taken out. The number it estimates, lambda, is roughly how much of what we failed to measure runs together across the city.

Clustered errors admit a second story: that incidents in one area raise incidents in the next. That model exists, and it is not offered here, because its coefficients cannot be read as ordinary effects and a page that printed them beside these would be teaching something false. Choosing between the two stories is a claim about how the world works, and the data cannot make it for you.

Watch the coefficients move. If income shifts a lot, the equation could not tell income apart from the other things that are smooth over the same parts of the city. Where the shops are. Where the trains stop. Who is about in the daytime. Where officers go.

Sometimes it overshoots. Across 996 areas the repair can push the clustering past zero and out the other side, which is a different problem, not a solved one.

Reports are counts of recorded incidents in the chosen category. Households is the number of private households in the area. Income is the median household income.

When areas are grouped, counts are added up and income is averaged across the areas being grouped, weighted by how many households each holds. An average of medians is not itself a median, so the income of a grouped area here is close to, but not the same as, the figure the census publishes for it. For census tracts the two agree closely, and the difference still moves R2 from 0.160 to 0.188.

Every set of areas here is built the same way, so that comparing them is a comparison of boundaries and nothing else.

Neighbours are

Moran's I asks whether neighbouring areas resemble each other, so it cannot be worked out until you say what a neighbour is. Nothing in the data answers that.

Touching counts areas that share a boundary or a corner. Touching, and theirs adds the neighbours of those neighbours. All, by 1/distance counts every area in the city, weighted by how far away it is, which is closer to what Tobler actually said: near things are more related, not that far things are unrelated.

Watch the number fall as the circle widens. Most of that is arithmetic rather than Vancouver: adding distant pairs that have nothing to do with each other dilutes whatever the near pairs were showing.

Notice what does not follow. Across 996 areas the number drops from 0.165 to 0.012, and the test still says it is nowhere near what chance would give. A small figure and a large one can be equally hard to explain away, because with a thousand areas even a faint pattern is easy to detect. So widening cannot be used to make the problem go away, and a value near zero is not permission to stop worrying.

There is no correct answer here, and that is the point. The number you report is a claim about what counts as near, and you should be able to defend it.

This page does what it is describing. It turns a city into boxes with numbers in them, and everything it cannot count is missing from it too. The 996 smallest areas stay drawn under every grouping, so what the grouping throws away is never quite off the screen.
