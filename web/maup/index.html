What the model misses
How much of the pattern the model explains (R2)
—

R2 runs from 0 to 1. It is the share of the variation between areas that the two predictors account for. At 0 the model tells you nothing; at 1 it would predict every area exactly.

A higher R2 does not mean a better description of the world. Changing the areas changes it, and there is no rule saying which way. Openshaw took 99 counties and grouped them into 6 regions in many different ways: depending only on which counties were put together, the correlation could be made to come out anywhere between −0.99 and +0.99.

For more, see: Openshaw, S. (1984) The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem. Concepts and Techniques in Modern Geography 38. Norwich: Geo Books. · Fotheringham, A. S. and Wong, D. W. S. (1991) The modifiable areal unit problem in multivariate statistical analysis. Environment and Planning A 23(7), 1025–1044.

The model

One equation for the whole city. It predicts the number of reported break-ins in an area from two things: how many private households the area holds, and the median household income there, in thousands of dollars.

Each number in front of a predictor says how much the prediction changes when that predictor goes up by one, holding the other still. A term is crossed out when it could easily have come out that size by chance (p is 0.05 or more), which is the usual reason to leave it out of the equation you write down.

The equation describes areas, not people. Nobody in this data is a person. Reading a coefficient as a fact about residents is the ecological fallacy, and it is one of the things this page is built to make visible.

The income term is where that goes wrong most easily. A negative coefficient does not say that people with lower incomes damage or steal more. It says areas whose residents report lower median incomes hold more records — and those areas also differ in what the land is used for, how many people pass through in a day, how much of the space is public, and how closely it is policed. Most of these incidents happen in streets, shops and parking spaces, not in the homes that the household figures count.

Are the errors clustered in space? (Moran's I)
—

The model's errors should be scattered. If areas where it guesses too low sit next to other areas where it guesses too low, something with a geography to it is missing from the model.

Moran's I measures that clustering: near 0 means scattered, positive means neighbours resemble each other. Neighbours here are areas that touch, each counted equally.

The usual reading of clustered errors is that a variable with a geography is missing from the model. Recording and policing are two such variables: where officers are sent, how readily an incident is reported, and how it is written up all vary across a city. Part of what clusters here is the recording rather than the thing recorded, and no amount of care with the statistics separates them.

The p-value comes from shuffling the errors between areas 999 times and asking how often chance alone beats what we see. Treat it as a rough guide: the errors are left over from a model already fitted to this data, so the shuffling is not quite testing what it appears to.

For more, see: Cliff, A. D. and Ord, J. K. (1972) Testing for spatial autocorrelation among regression residuals. Geographical Analysis 4(3), 267–284. · Cliff, A. D. and Ord, J. K. (1973) Spatial Autocorrelation. London: Pion.

Cut the city into

Dissemination areas and census tracts are real units published by Statistics Canada. A tract is built from whole dissemination areas, so the two are the same city counted at two grains.

The random areas are grown from random starting points until each holds about the same number of households, which is roughly how the real units are built. They are drawn only to answer one question: if the number of areas stays the same and only the boundaries move, does the answer move too?

It does. That is the modifiable areal unit problem, and it has two halves. Going from 996 areas to 118 is the scale problem. Pressing redraw, with the number of areas held still, is the aggregation problem, now more often called the zoning effect.

For more, see: Openshaw, S. and Taylor, P. J. (1979) A million or so correlation coefficients: three experiments on the modifiable areal unit problem. In N. Wrigley (ed.) Statistical Applications in the Spatial Sciences. London: Pion, 127–144.

Reports of

These are counts of incidents reported to police and recorded under each heading, in 2015 and 2016. That is not the same as things that happened. A record exists only if someone reported it, an officer wrote it down, and it was filed under this heading, and none of those three steps happens at the same rate everywhere in a city or for every kind of incident.

Compare the categories and you are partly comparing how willingly each is reported. A stolen bicycle is often not reported at all; a break-in at a business usually is, because a claim needs a file number. Any map of these counts is a map of records.

The headings are legal and administrative categories, not descriptions of behaviour. Mischief in Canadian law covers damage to property, graffiti included; whether an act is written up under that heading depends on who complains, whose property was involved, and how an officer files it.

A record also marks where police were. Officers are not spread evenly across a city, and an incident becomes a record where there is someone to record it. So these counts measure police activity as well as anything else.

Residential break and enter is deliberately missing. It is the category the GEOG 370 lab asks you to model, and this page is meant to sharpen what you make of your own results, not to hand them to you.

The model

Each area on its own fits one equation from an area's own households and income. Its errors are clustered in space, which says something with a geography is missing.

Areas plus their neighbours is the standard repair. It adds the average count in the surrounding areas as a third predictor. Because that predictor is tangled up with the thing being predicted, it cannot be fitted by ordinary least squares; it is estimated in two stages, using the neighbours' households and income, and their neighbours' in turn, to stand in for it.

It works, in the narrow sense. Watch Moran's I fall. But look at what the model now says: an area's count is predicted by the counts around it. That is the statistical form of the reasoning behind hot-spot and predictive policing, and it is worth being uneasy about. Records are made where officers are; a model that sends officers where records are will be right about the records.

Fixing the statistics does not change what the numbers are or what they are used for. That the map can be improved is not the same as the map being made answerable.

Left map shows

Reports are counts of recorded incidents in the chosen category. Households is the number of private households in the area. Income is the median household income.

When areas are grouped, counts are added up and income is averaged across the areas being grouped, weighted by how many households each holds. An average of medians is not itself a median, so the income of a grouped area here is close to, but not the same as, the figure the census publishes for it. For census tracts the two agree closely, and the difference still moves R2 from 0.160 to 0.188.

Every set of areas here is built the same way, so that comparing them is a comparison of boundaries and nothing else.

Right map shows

What the model misses is the residual: reported break-ins minus the number the equation predicts, divided by the spread of those differences. Red is more break-ins reported than predicted, blue is fewer.

A red area can mean three different things and the map cannot tell them apart: more incidents happening, more of them being reported, or more police attention turning what happens into a record. This is a map of records, and none of the three is a statement about the people who live there.

Maps of this kind are also not only descriptions. They are used to decide where patrols go, and patrols produce records, so a crime map takes part in making the pattern it appears to be reporting. Geographers who study policing treat that loop as the main thing to notice about crime mapping, rather than as a flaw in the data to be corrected.

Share of reports is a location quotient: the area's share of the city's reports divided by its share of the city's households. One means exactly the share its households would suggest, two means twice that share.
