How much of the pattern your zones explain
—
—

R2 runs from 0 to 1. It is the share of the differences between zones that the straight line accounts for. At 0 the line tells you nothing about where a zone sits; at 1 it would put every zone exactly on it. Nothing in it says one thing causes the other, and it has no sign: which way the line leans is the slope beside it.

Grouping areas together usually pushes it up, because averaging removes the differences inside each group and leaves the differences between groups. Here it is 0.17 across the 1,317 areas and 0.19 across the 190 published census tracts, which is the expected direction.

Usually is not always. The four eight-zone rules on offer give 0.67, 0.14, 0.09 and 0.03. Three of them are below the areas themselves, and two of them draw a line that leans the other way. Same ground, same records, same number of zones, and the only thing that changed is where the lines go. You can do better than all four.

Openshaw and Taylor took the 99 counties of Iowa and searched for groupings into 6 regions. They could drive the correlation between two variables anywhere from −0.99 to +0.99 by choosing where the lines went. They called it applied gerrymandering.

For more, see: Openshaw, S. and Taylor, P. J. (1979) A million or so correlation coefficients: three experiments on the modifiable areal unit problem. In N. Wrigley (ed.) Statistical Applications in the Spatial Sciences. London: Pion, 127–144. · Fotheringham, A. S. and Wong, D. W. S. (1991) The modifiable areal unit problem in multivariate statistical analysis. Environment and Planning A 23(7), 1025–1044.

The line’s slope
—
—

How much greenness the line adds for every extra $10,000 of median household income. It is a property of the zones you drew, not of any household.

Watch it and R2 separately. They are not the same thing and they do not move together: a zoning can tilt the line without tightening it, and it can tighten without tilting. Across the 1,317 areas themselves the slope is 0.018 per $10,000. Two of the four rules give 0.06 to 0.09, three to five times that; two lean the other way, at −0.029 and −0.039.

Nothing here is about a person. Reading a line between areas as a statement about the people in them is the ecological fallacy.

Start from

The four eight-zone starts are each drawn by a rule somebody could state before knowing what it would do: same number of households in each, compact lumps, whole census tracts grouped together, the city’s own neighbourhoods grouped together. Each is named for its rule and for nothing else, and its R2 and its slope print beside the maps once you choose it.

They are not the extremes. Forty zonings were drawn from stated rules, ten for each of four things a preset is here to show, and the one kept from each ten is the best of its own ten rather than the best of the forty. There is room to beat all four. What the four are for is the plain fact that they disagree.

Dissemination areas and census tracts are real, published by Statistics Canada, and cannot be painted over. Somebody drew each of them, for a purpose, and none of the purposes was this question. A dissemination area is built to hold 400 to 700 people and to be compact; a tract is built from whole dissemination areas.

Painting changes eight zones, not a thousand. Eight is what two people can redraw in five minutes, and holding the count still is what makes the comparison about where the lines are.

Choose a zone colour, then click or drag across areas on either map to move them into it. The two maps are one zoning drawn twice, so a stroke on either moves the same areas and every number on the page updates as you go.

With a keyboard: put focus on either map, move between areas with the arrow keys, and press 1 to 8 to move the area under the cursor into that zone.

A zone can end up in two pieces. The widget lets it, and says so under the map, because whether a zone has to be in one piece is a rule somebody chose rather than something the data knows. Census units are built connected; electoral districts are supposed to be; the searched zonings here were kept connected on purpose so that they could not win by cheating in a way the real thing cannot.

Income for a zone is

Greenness has no choice to make. It is a value per square metre, so a zone's greenness is the average over its pixels, and it comes out the same however the pixels are grouped. Nothing is lost.

Income does have a choice, and there is no right answer to it. What Statistics Canada publishes for an area is a median: the middle household. Medians do not add up. The middle household of two areas put together is not the average of the two middles, and it cannot be worked out from them at all.

So a zone's income here is one of three averages of the areas' medians: a plain one, one weighted by households, or one weighted by people. They disagree, the line moves when you switch, and none of the three is the figure Statistics Canada would publish for the merged zone.

That is worth more than it looks. A number can move because you redrew the boundaries and it can move because you changed how you added it up, and on this page you can see both happen to the same map. On the reversed zoning it is not a small move. On one of the four rules the line is flat when income is weighted by households and clearly tilted on a plain average of the very same eight zones, with not one boundary moved. The size of a finding, and sometimes its direction, is decided by a choice nobody usually writes down.

For the classroom

On your own, one minute. Across the 1,317 areas, R2 between income and greenness is 0.17. You are about to group those areas into eight zones. Write down one number: what will R2 be then? Write it before anybody presses anything.

In pairs, two minutes. Compare your two numbers and say why they differ. Then press each of the four starting rules and read R2 off each. Now paint. There are four prizes: the steepest positive slope, the steepest negative slope, the highest R2, and the R2 nearest zero. Type a name, press Copy link, and hand the link on with your slope and your R2.

Back to the room, two minutes. Take the four prizes in turn and open the winning link at the front. Most people write a number above 0.17, because grouping is supposed to strengthen a relationship, and two of the four rules are below it. Some write a number close to 0.17, on the view that a zone is a sample of the city; a zone is not a sample, it is a sum. Almost nobody expects a zoning whose line leans the other way, and two of the four lean that way. Then ask what the prizes cannot settle: which of these zonings would anybody put in a report, and how would a reader of that report ever tell?
